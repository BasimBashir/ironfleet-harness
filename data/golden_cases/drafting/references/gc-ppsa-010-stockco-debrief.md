Debrief: Court of Appeal decision on the heifer sale-and-leaseback

Outcome. The appeal is dismissed. The Court of Appeal in Stockco Ltd v Gibson [2012] NZCA 330 held that
the banks' security interest continued in the heifers despite the sale to you, so the banks' all-assets
charge ranks ahead of your interest. Costs were reserved.

The test. Personal Property Securities Act 1999, s 53 lets a buyer of goods sold in the ordinary course of
business of the seller take them free of the seller's security interest. The Court adopted the two stage
process from ORIX New Zealand Ltd v Milne: first identify what the seller's business actually was, then ask
whether the disposition was of a kind customarily made in that business. The focus is the seller's business,
not yours.

Why the sale failed it. The seller was a dairy farming group, not a livestock trader or financier. Selling
its core herd in a single transaction, well below market value, against the banks' refusal of consent, with
the proceeds applied to a land settlement and the animals never leaving the farms, fell a long way outside
the ordinary course of the seller's business. The s 53 protection was therefore unavailable.

What to change. Before buying stock from a borrower of another lender, (1) obtain the incumbent secured
party's written consent or release, or a subordination agreement; (2) treat any discount to market value,
one-off bulk disposal, or immediate leaseback to the seller group as a red flag that s 53 will not protect
you; and (3) do not rely on prompt registration of your own financing statement to defeat an earlier perfected
interest the sale did not shed.

The liquidators can recover $480,000; the investor has a value defence only to the $780,000 he paid in.

Paying money is a "transaction" under Companies Act 1993, s 292(3)(e) and a "disposition" under the
Property Law Act 2007 whether or not the money was the company's own. The payment came from a co-mingled
fund of misappropriated client money, so the argument that trust money lies outside the insolvent estate
fails: the fraud made the investor a creditor as well as a beneficiary, and the payment was an insolvent transaction
made when the manager could not pay its due debts and enabled him to receive more than he would in the
liquidation. It is therefore voidable and liable to be set aside.

The defences fix the amount. Under Companies Act 1993, s 296(3), and the matching valuable consideration
limb of Property Law Act 2007, s 349(1), the recipient must show that he gave value in the reasonably held
belief that the payment would stand, and the value must be real and substantial. He never parted with
beneficial ownership of the $780,000, but he did part with it absolutely, so the return of that capital is
value for the payment; the fictitious balance of about $480,000 is not, and the payment is bifurcated
accordingly.

The change of position limb fails: the development spending is referable to the whole receipt and the
contemporaneous documents show the money was sought to reduce existing debt, so no detriment was incurred
in reliance on the payment's validity.

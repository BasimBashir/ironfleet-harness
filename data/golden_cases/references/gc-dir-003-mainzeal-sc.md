Yes: the directors breached s 136. Companies Act 1993, s 136 operates on the premise that
directors should not agree to a company incurring obligations unless confident on reasonable grounds
that they will be honoured. "Believes on reasonable grounds" requires more than hope and less than
absolute conviction, and the belief is judged objectively on the grounds the directors actually had.

The provision is not confined to obligations of a particular kind. It is not limited to transactions
on capital account or to a single major commitment approved by the board itself: agreement to the
continuation of trading extends to the incurring of obligations that are the inevitable corollary of
that continuation, including ordinary trading obligations entered into by executives under delegated
authority. The section may be invoked in relation to a course of trading.

That is the approach of the Court of Appeal, adopted by the Supreme Court: Yan v Mainzeal [2021] NZCA 99.
Section 136 falls to be construed by reference to the ordinary meaning of its statutory text and is
not read down merely because there is some cross-over with s 135.

Because the directors knew the company could not pay its debts as they fell due, yet agreed that it
should keep operating as usual without addressing that position, there were no reasonable grounds for
believing that medium to long-term obligations would be met, so liability attaches to the obligations
incurred after the breach date.

The coins are not the company's property. They are held on trust for the accountholders and are
outside the liquidation.

Directions should be sought under s 284 of the Companies Act 1993. Companies Act 1993, s 2 defines
"property" broadly, to include real and personal property and any right or interest in it. New Zealand
courts read that definition widely: in McIntosh v Fisk [2017] NZSC 78 the Supreme Court accepted that
a payment of money fell within the insolvency provisions because of the width of the s 2 definition.

Cryptocurrency satisfies the classic criteria of property in
National Provincial Bank Ltd v Ainsworth [1965] 1 AC 1175: definable subject matter, identifiable by
third parties, capable of assumption by third parties, and having some degree of permanence or
stability. A coin is definable by reference to its public key, controlled through the private key, and
is capable of transfer - so it is intangible property and can form the subject matter of a trust.

An express trust arose for each type of cryptocurrency, with all accountholders holding that currency
as beneficiaries and the exchange as bare trustee holding the private keys and following their
instructions. Certainty of subject matter and objects are met by the exchange's ledger of coin
balances, and the intention to create a trust appears from the terms and fee model.

The consequence is that the wallets are not assets of the company distributable to creditors: had they
been, they would have been realised and distributed under Part 16 with the accountholders ranking only
as unsecured creditors. Accounts whose holders cannot be identified fall under s 76 of the Trustee Act
1956 rather than being retained by the company.

The Comptroller is paid first out of the proceeds of sale, and the receivers' s 34 application succeeds.

The starting point is that Customs and Excise Act 1996, s 97(1) makes the duty on imported goods a statutory
charge on the goods until fully paid, whether or not property has passed to a third party; the only exception
is a purchaser for value without knowledge of the unpaid duty, which the bank is not. Its timing point, that
its interest attached and was perfected before importation, is a PPSA argument and does not meet the charge.

Personal Property Securities Act 1999, s 23 is what takes the contest outside that Act: by s 23(b) a charge
created by another enactment is not a security interest, so the priority rules of the PPSA are never engaged
and first-in-time reasoning is unavailable; nor is the earlier specific provision impliedly repealed.

The 1996 Act then supplies its own order. The chief executive may take possession of charged goods and sell
them in satisfaction of the charge, and in a receivership he must notify the receiver that the duty is a
charge; regulations made under the Act fix the manner and order of priority for dispersal of the proceeds.

Terminals (NZ) Ltd v Comptroller of Customs [2013] NZSC 139 confirms taxing statutes are construed like any
other, by text and purpose. Duty is the price of bringing goods into New Zealand, and a prior-attaching
interest able to defeat it would undermine the deferred-payment accommodation the importer used.

So the bank takes only the balance: the statutory charge has priority over its security interest.

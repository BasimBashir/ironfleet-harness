The directors are liable in reckless trading. Under Companies Act 1993, s 135 the business was
carried on in a manner likely to create a substantial risk of serious loss to creditors: the company
traded while balance sheet insolvent, using creditors' funds as working capital, on support from the
wider group that was never binding.
The essential pillars of the section, set out in Mason v Lewis [2006] 3 NZLR 225, are that the duty
is owed to the company rather than to any particular creditors, that the test is objective and
directed to the manner in which the business is carried on, and that a sober and ongoing assessment
must distinguish legitimate from illegitimate business risk.
Three cumulative considerations established the breach: trading while balance sheet insolvent, the
intercompany debt not being recoverable in reality; no assurance of group support on which the
directors could reasonably rely if adverse circumstances arose; and poor trading performance prone to
significant one-off losses, so a strong capital base or equivalent backing was needed to survive. The
informal expressions of support were a mere assumption of support and gave creditors inadequate
protection.
The section is not limited to trading a company that is hopelessly insolvent in a cash flow sense: a
substantial risk of serious loss can arise where a substantial company is traded in a way that leaves
it vulnerable to collapse with a serious deficiency on liquidation. Normal business risks stay
outside the section; this was illegitimate risk taking, so liability attaches, the breach running
from 1 March 2010, the date the directors knew the company's liabilities exceeded its assets.

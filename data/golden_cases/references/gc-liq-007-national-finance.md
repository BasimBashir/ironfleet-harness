The trustee is right that the statutory claim fails, but wrong that nothing else stands. Two heads of
liability run in the company's favour and so can be pursued by its liquidators.

First, the reasonable diligence duties inserted in the trust deed are, under the statute,
exclusively for the protection of investors. The statutory purpose is investor protection, the company is
not within the limited class Parliament intended to give a private right of action, and the claim in
breach of statutory duty is therefore struck out.

Secondly, the trust deed is a contract, so the trustee owed the company a contractual duty to exercise
reasonable diligence in ascertaining and remedying breaches of the deed. Liabilities arising under other
rules of law are preserved by Securities Act 1978, s 65, and that is also the footing for the third head:
a concurrent duty of care in tort. Its basis is the assumption of responsibility by a party with special
skills providing services on which the company relies, within a contractual relationship that supplies
the requisite proximity, following Henderson v Merrett Syndicates Ltd [1995] 2 AC 145.

The trustee's defences fail. The "author of its own misfortune" objection does not apply: the trustee had
itself assumed responsibility to the company for ascertaining whether the deed had been breached, just as
the auditors had, so the company's own breaches are no defence. Policy considerations also support the
duty: a duty to the issuer does not erode the protection given to investors, it enhances trustee
accountability once the failed company passes to insolvency practitioners, and it would be incongruous if
the company could sue its directors and auditors over breaches of the deed but not its trustee. The
trustee's indemnity in the deed is not a complete answer, since an indemnity is unavailable for liability
incurred through lack of due care.

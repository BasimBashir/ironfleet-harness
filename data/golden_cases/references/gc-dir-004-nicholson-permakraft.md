Yes — on these facts the director is liable to the company, and the unanimous assent does not save him.

Directors' duties are owed to the company, but where a company is insolvent or near-insolvent,
or the contemplated payment would jeopardise its solvency, creditors are entitled to
consideration: Nicholson v Permakraft (NZ) Ltd [1985] 1 NZLR 242. Cooke J there held that in a
situation of marginal commercial solvency directors must consider whether what they do will
prejudice the company's practical ability to discharge promptly debts owed to current and likely
continuing trade creditors, adopting an objective test from In re Horsley & Weight Ltd — a payment
to the prejudice of those creditors when loss ought to have been appreciated is capable of being
misfeasance for which the director must make good the company's loss.

Informal unanimous assent will cure defects in the internal machinery for declaring a payment
(the Re Duomatic Ltd principle, which saved the dividend in Nicholson itself), but it does not
answer the separate question of prejudice: the company is not prevented from complaining where
creditors have been prejudiced by a payment made when it could not pay its due debts.

The company received no consideration for the $48,000, which discharged the director's personal
debt while trade creditors went unpaid, so the director is liable to repay it to the liquidator.

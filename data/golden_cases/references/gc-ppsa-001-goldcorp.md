The investors have no proprietary claim to the bullion: they rank only as unsecured creditors behind
the bank's floating charge.

Property cannot pass under a contract for the sale of unascertained goods until goods answering the
description are ascertained and appropriated to the contract: Sale of Goods Act 1908, s 18. Nothing was
ever set aside for these buyers, so neither legal nor equitable title passed on the sale itself.
Goldcorp Exchange Ltd v Liggett [1994] UKPC 3 so holds: a simple contract for the sale of unascertained
goods passes no equitable title, adopting Atkin LJ's reasoning in In re Wait.

The storage promises do not cure that. They gave the buyer no entitlement to separate appropriation of
any goods, so they were no declaration of trust; and since no goods were ever held for any buyer there
was nothing to found a bailment either - "there was never any bailment, and no identifiable property to
which any trust could attach". An estoppel gives no title, and cannot prevail against a third party
holding a real proprietary interest in property that actually exists.

Nor can the investors trace into the price: total failure of consideration arises only when delivery is
demanded and refused, so no proprietary interest arises in the purchase monies. They prove personal
claims and share rateably with the other unsecured creditors; the floating charge takes the bullion.

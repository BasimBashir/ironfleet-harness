The company will recover its core loss of $210,000, but only a small part of the $95,000 of
administration costs.

Compensation is fixed under Companies Act 1993, s 301, which requires the amount the court thinks just
in the light of the director's conduct. The starting point is the deterioration in the company's
financial position between the breach becoming evident and the liquidation, and the court then applies
the three factors of causation, culpability and the duration of the trading.

Shaw v Owens [2017] NZCA 315 decides that the cost of running the liquidation is a separate question.
Those costs are generally incurred regardless of whether the directors are liable, and liquidators are
entitled to follow an investigative course, but they cannot necessarily expect to recover more than their
usual legal costs if successful. The purpose of a s 301 award is to recoup the company's loss attributable
to the breaches, so an allowance for the liquidators' costs can be made only where they were necessarily
incurred as a consequence of the breach, and the award must stay
proportionate to the company's actual loss.

Here the bulk of the $95,000 went on an unsuccessful thesis that the directors had set the company up to
defeat creditors, and on work directed at recovering for a single creditor - a debt-collection exercise
rather than the general costs of liquidating the company. On a broad-brush basis the directors should be
liable for only a proportion of those costs, the balance falling on the liquidation. Diluting the
creditors' recovery with liquidation costs of that scale would also make the pursuit commercially
irrational, which the court will weigh against the liquidators.

No: Companies Act 1993, s 298 does not apply to these distributions, and recovery from the trustees fails.

Was the undertaking held on trust? Yes. A company may declare itself trustee of its own property, and a trust
of personal property may be constituted informally. Milroy v Lord requires everything necessary to be done by
one of three modes, transfer to trustees, transfer to beneficiaries, or a self-declaration, and refuses to
perfect an intended transfer by recasting it as a declaration of trust. That does not defeat this trust:
nothing was ever transferred, so there is no failed transfer to rescue. The directors resolved to trade as a
trust and then paid the trading profits away as the beneficiaries' income, from which conduct a declaration
of trust is inferred; absent resolutions are not fatal, because the directors' assent binds the company.

Secondly, what was distributed was not company property available to creditors, because trust property is not
company property. The only interest retained was the right of indemnity against the trust property for
expenses properly incurred as trustee.
Octavo Investments Pty Ltd v Knight (1979) 27 ALR 129 is the authority for that limitation, and the extent of
the indemnity had to be proved by the liquidators, the onus lying on them to show the company was out of
pocket when each distribution was made, which was not shown.

Thirdly, the subsection reaches only dispositions where the directors are bound, in the company's interests,
to obtain fair value for what goes out. Paying a trust's own income to its beneficiaries through its corporate
trustee is not such a transaction, however inadequate the consideration.

The distributions are therefore not recoverable under s 298(2); the claim must be reframed as breach of duty
by the directors, or quantified on the indemnity.

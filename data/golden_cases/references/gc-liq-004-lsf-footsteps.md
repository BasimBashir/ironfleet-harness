No. The company cannot recover the $280,000 from the new trustee as a debt.

The right of indemnity for liabilities incurred as trustee survives removal as trustee and vests in the
company's liquidator, and it is secured by an equitable lien over the trust assets: Trustee Act 1956, s 38
recognises the trustee's right to reimburse himself out of trust property, and the lien arises by
operation of law, confers a proprietary interest and takes priority over the beneficiaries. Because the
trust assets now vest in the new trustee, the lien follows them.

But an equitable lien is a charge, not a personal covenant. Absent a contractual indemnity in the deed of
removal, the new trustee is under no personal duty to pay the trust debts; the relationship is one of
power and liability, not claim and duty. The lien is enforceable only by judicial process - an application
to the court for an order for sale of the units or for the appointment of a receiver - and not by
foreclosure or a private sale, and not by a money claim against the new trustee. There is accordingly no
debtor-creditor relationship between the two companies.

That is fatal to the statutory demand. Companies Act 1993, s 290 allows a demand to be set aside where
there is a genuine dispute as to the debt: because the company is not a creditor of the new trustee, the
claimed debt is genuinely disputable, and the demand will be set aside. The liquidators' remaining
remedies are to enforce the lien by judicial process, and to investigate whether the transfer of the
units to the new trustee can itself be set aside as a transaction at an undervalue or to defeat
creditors.

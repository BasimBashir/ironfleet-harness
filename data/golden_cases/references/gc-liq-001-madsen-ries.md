Yes. The director is liable in reckless trading under Companies Act 1993, s 135.

Section 135 prohibits a director from agreeing to, or causing or allowing, the business to be carried on
in a manner likely to create a substantial risk of serious loss to the company's creditors. The section
is forward-looking: the risk is assessed when the decision is made, not by reference to whether the GST
was then a current liability.

On the Supreme Court's reasoning in Madsen-Ries v Cooper [2020] NZSC 100, where continued trading will
result in a shortfall to creditors and the company is not salvageable, continued trading breaches s 135.
Here the director knew from November 2019 that the GST shortfall was certain, so the loss was a certainty
rather than a mere risk, and $415,000 is plainly a serious loss.

It is no answer that finishing and selling the houses was a sensible commercial decision likely to leave
some creditors better off: there is no comparative exercise between immediate liquidation and continued
trading, and creditors cannot be compartmentalised so that one of them carries the whole loss. Statutory
priorities and the pari passu principle are part of the scheme the section protects.

The director should have stopped trading, or used a formal mechanism (a compromise under Part 14,
voluntary administration under Part 15A) or an informal mechanism under which all affected creditors,
including the Commissioner, were consulted and agreed. Having done neither, he is in breach of s 135,
and there is also a breach of s 136 because the GST debts were incurred knowing they would not be paid.
Any s 301 relief must respond to those breaches, on a compensatory or restitutionary basis.

No. Your client did not take the heifers free of the banks' charge.

Personal Property Securities Act 1999, s 53(1) protects a buyer of goods sold in the ordinary course of
business of the seller: such a buyer takes the goods free of a security interest given by the seller even
where the security agreement prohibits the disposition. The statutory focus is the seller's business, not
the buyer's.

The two-stage approach from ORIX New Zealand Ltd v Milne [2007] 3 NZLR 637 applies: identify what the
seller's business in fact was, then ask whether the disposition was of a kind customarily made in it. The
seller was a dairy farming group whose ordinary course of business was producing milk and on-selling stock
in the normal run of dairy farming, not livestock trading or financing.

Measured against that, the sale was a long way outside the ordinary course of business: a one-off
sale-and-leaseback of the group's core herd, at well below market value, settled in a single day without
consent after the banks had refused it, with proceeds applied to a land settlement, and the animals never
leaving the seller's farms. Those features make it highly unusual, not a customary farming disposition.

Because the sale was not in the ordinary course, the banks' security interest continued in the heifers
notwithstanding the sale and your client's registration, and their charge ranks ahead.

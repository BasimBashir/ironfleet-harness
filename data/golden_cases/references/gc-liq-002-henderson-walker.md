Henderson v Walker [2019] NZHC 2184 is directly in point: the director has a claim in breach of
confidence, and probably invasion of privacy, but not misfeasance in public office, breach of statutory
duty or conversion.

Breach of confidence. The elements are those in Coco v AN Clarke (Engineers) Ltd [1969] RPC 41: the
information must have the necessary quality of confidence; it must have been imparted in circumstances
importing an obligation of confidence; and there must be an unauthorised use of it. The director's
personal emails and tax records satisfy the first element even though they were intermingled with
company material on the laptop, and a liquidator who takes possession of a company's records for the
purposes of the liquidation is fixed with that obligation. Handing them to a commercial investigator and
to a government department for purposes unconnected with the liquidation is use outside the liquidator's
function. Damages are assessed on the tortious measure and, for personal information, general damages
for distress are available; the award in Henderson v Walker was modest, scaled to the disclosure.

Misfeasance in public office. This fails. A liquidator is not a public officer: although the office is
created and regulated by statute, liquidation is a process internal to a company's affairs, the
liquidator is paid out of the asset pool rather than by the state, and the creditors, shareholders and
directors affected do not deal with him in any public capacity. The tort also requires proof of damage
beyond humiliation and distress.

Breach of statutory duty also fails: Part 16 of the Companies Act 1993 confers positive powers and
duties, and does not impose a negative duty of confidentiality, so the remedy for misuse of documents
lies in private law, not in statute.

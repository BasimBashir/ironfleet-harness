Yes: the Commissioner's preferential claim is paid out of both components of the fund ahead of the bank.

The regime is Companies Act 1993, s 312 with Schedule 7: preferential claims, which include GST, are payable
out of accounts receivable and inventory, or their proceeds, ahead of a security interest over those classes,
so the case turns on the meaning of the term. Schedule 7 cl 2(2) adopts the PPSA definition:
Personal Property Securities Act 1999, s 16 makes an account receivable a monetary obligation not evidenced
by chattel paper, an investment security or a negotiable instrument, whether or not earned by performance.

The narrower view is Commissioner of Inland Revenue v Northshore Tavern Ltd (2008) 23 NZTC 22,074, which held
the phrase confined to book debts. It should not be followed. The excluded forms of evidence would be
surplusage if only book debts were meant; "whether or not it has been earned by performance" reaches
obligations not yet due; and the history shows the term was chosen to displace "book debt" because
computerised records made the adjective misleading, with no clearly expressed limiting intention. A judge of
co-ordinate jurisdiction may depart from an earlier first-instance decision satisfied that it is wrong.

Applying the broad meaning, both components qualify. The council's obligation to repay the contributions is a
chose in action for a money sum arising before liquidation, and classification is assessed as at that date; the
trust account credit is an obligation to pay the company on demand. Neither is a trade debt.

Each is therefore a monetary obligation within Schedule 7, and the Commissioner is paid first, with the
liquidators' costs of gathering and distributing the fund to be met before either claimant.

The liquidators may stand in the trustee's shoes but only to the extent of real trust liabilities, and they rank behind the mortgagee.

A corporate trustee has a right of indemnity against trust property for liabilities properly incurred
in that capacity, and exercising it gives rise to an equitable lien protected by caveat: Octavo Investments Pty Ltd v Knight (1979) 144 CLR 360,
applied in New Zealand for liquidator standing (Re Suco Gold). Because
that lien can defeat a beneficiary who is subrogated to the trustee's rights (Hardoon v Belilios [1901] AC 118),
the court scrutinises whether the liabilities are genuine.

Entries merely recorded as current liabilities in accounts prepared years before liquidation are not enough:
no creditor has claimed, and liabilities that are neither owing nor enforceable do not support the exercise of
the right of indemnity. Only the IRD debt and costs reasonably incurred qua trustee are recoverable on that basis.

Even where a lien exists, it does not displace a prior-ranking mortgage securing obligations granted for the
company's accommodation. Because such a mortgage stands ahead of the company's equitable lien, the mortgagee
takes priority in the sale proceeds and the company is left with only a surplus (if any) after the mortgage
debt is met.
